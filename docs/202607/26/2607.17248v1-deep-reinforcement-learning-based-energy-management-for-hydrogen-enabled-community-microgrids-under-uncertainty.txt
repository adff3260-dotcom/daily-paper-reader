Abstract 

Community microgrids that incorporate hydrogen can increase the use of locally generated renewable electricity and strengthen supply resilience. Their real-time operation is nevertheless difficult because renewable output, household demand, electricity tariffs, and grid availability vary over time, while batteries and hydrogen storage respond over different time scales. This study presents a proximal policy optimization (PPO) energy management system (EMS) for a grid-connected community microgrid comprising photo-voltaic and wind generation, a battery, an electrolyzer, a hydrogen tank, a fuel cell, and diesel standby generation. The dispatch task is expressed as a Markov decision process with 11 state variables and three continuous com-mands governing battery, diesel, and hydrogen-pathway operation; utility-grid exchange closes the remaining power imbalance. Performance is assessed using 8,760 hourly records for a 1,000-household community in Rockhamp-ton, Australia. In the normal case, the learned policy delivered A$195,690.67 of net annual operating revenue, a renewable fraction of 91.2%, a carbon in-tensity of 0.085 kg CO 2/kWh within the adopted accounting boundary, and

Raising the hourly outage probability from 1% to 5% reduced revenue to A$169,892.21 and demand satisfaction to 98.79%; battery discharge and diesel generation increased by approximately 413% and 429%, respectively. The findings indicate that PPO can coordinate battery and hydrogen resources effectively, while also revealing sensitivity to renewable-profile construction, policy-training stability, and the selected evaluation boundary.

1. Introduction 

1.1. Context 

A microgrid creates a local coordination layer between distributed gener-ation, energy storage, flexible demand, and the utility connection. By man-aging these resources within a defined electrical boundary, it can improve supply reliability and increase the share of electricity served by low-carbon assets close to consumers [1, 2, 3]. In residential communities, this role extends beyond the familiar PV–battery arrangement. Current designs may combine photovoltaic (PV) arrays, wind turbines, battery energy storage sys-tems (BESSs), electric vehicles, demand response, and hydrogen technologies within a single multi-energy platform [4, 5, 6, 7]. The two storage media serve different operational purposes. Batteries respond efficiently to rapid imbalances, making them well suited to intraday smoothing, peak reduction, and increased local use of renewable generation. Their cost and finite energy capacity, however, can limit their suitability for lengthy renewable shortfalls or energy shifting over extended periods. A hydrogen chain offers a complementary option: surplus electricity drives an electrolyzer, the resulting hydrogen is retained in a tank, and a fuel cell later converts the stored fuel back to electricity [8, 9]. The practical value of this pathway depends on disciplined management of conversion losses, equipment ratings, and storage limits [10, 11]. A hybrid battery–hydrogen arrangement can therefore assign fast balancing to the BESS while reserving hydrogen for slower or longer-duration support. Coordinating these assets is complicated by uncertainty. Solar and wind availability vary with weather, household demand changes with occupant be-2haviour, and both tariffs and grid status can differ from one dispatch interval to the next. These variations make real-time balancing difficult and weaken control strategies based on fixed operating priorities [12, 13, 14]. Australian residential studies have shown that PV, wind, battery, grid, diesel, and hy-drogen combinations can reduce operating cost and emissions, although the achievable benefit is strongly linked to the quality of the EMS used under changing conditions [15, 16, 17]. An effective community-scale controller must consequently coordinate intermittent generation, uncertain demand, short-duration electrical storage, and the longer-duration hydrogen pathway as one coupled decision problem. 

1.2. Rationale for Adaptive Control 

Most conventional microgrid controllers fall into three broad groups: rule-based dispatch, deterministic scheduling, and repeated numerical optimiza-tion. Rule-based methods are attractive because their logic is transparent and inexpensive to execute, but predetermined priorities cannot fully reflect rapid changes in demand, renewable availability, price, and stored energy [18, 19, 20]. Deterministic and metaheuristic optimizers can deliver im-proved cost–emission or cost–reliability trade-offs, yet their results remain dependent on forecasts and scenario assumptions, and a new optimization problem is generally solved whenever conditions change [21, 22, 23]. These burdens grow as the controller must simultaneously manage PV, wind, a battery, an electrolyzer, a hydrogen tank, a fuel cell, backup generation, and grid transactions. Reviews of contemporary EMS research increasingly emphasise un-certainty treatment, forecasting, computational intelligence, and adaptive scheduling for renewable-rich systems with several storage technologies [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. In the related Australian real-time study, particle swarm optimization (PSO) improved on a fixed rule-based controller (RBC), but PSO still requires repeated solution of an optimization problem and does not retain a reusable control policy learned from earlier operation [17]. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) provides an alternative formulation in which an agent observes the current system, applies a dispatch action, receives a reward that reflects performance, and gradually improves a policy through interaction. Evidence from other energy domains supports this direction. Expert-assisted reinforcement learning has improved data efficiency in electrified-vehicle EMS applications, while model-predictive-control-supported DRL has 3been used for fuel-cell/battery systems with adaptability and degradation considerations [29, 30]. Multi-agent and safety-constrained DRL have also been applied to building demand response and comfort management [31, 32]. Other examples include coordinated port logistics and energy operation, regenerative-braking storage, PV-aware urban rail control, and joint power allocation with computing-task offloading [33, 34, 35, 36]. These studies demonstrate the suitability of DRL for sequential decisions with nonlinear dynamics and uncertainty, but their operating constraints differ materially from those of a residential community microgrid that combines batteries and hydrogen storage. 

1.3. Unresolved Research Problem 

Although the literature is moving toward data-driven and uncertainty-aware EMS design, reviews continue to identify shortcomings in scalability, integrated storage control, sector-specific validation, and robust operation under uncertainty [6, 4, 37]. Existing DRL work is often centred on vehicles, buildings, transport infrastructure, or communication systems. Those appli-cations do not jointly represent the residential demand profile, grid exchange, diesel backup, and the full electrolyzer–tank–fuel-cell pathway considered in this study [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 36, 35]. The authors’ earlier Australian investigations provide the planning and control basis for the present work. The planning studies examined the techno-economic and environmental implications of PV–wind–battery–diesel–grid– hydrogen configurations, but focused on capacity selection and scenario as-sessment rather than a policy for adaptive hourly operation [15, 16]. The subsequent real-time study compared RBC and PSO dispatch, establishing the benefit of active coordination but retaining a predefined or repeatedly optimized decision process [17]. The remaining need is therefore a learning-based EMS that jointly schedules the battery, diesel generator, electrolyzer, hydrogen inventory, fuel cell, and grid exchange for a residential community while responding to renewable, load, tariff, and outage uncertainty. 

1.4. Study Contributions 

The specific contributions are as follows: 

• A continuous-control EMS based on PPO is constructed for a hydrogen-integrated community microgrid subject to varying renewable generation, household demand, tariffs, storage states, and grid availability. 4• Battery and hydrogen operation are coordinated through one policy. The three action channels determine battery power, diesel output, and the di-rection and magnitude of hydrogen-pathway operation, while grid import or export is obtained from the remaining power imbalance. 

• The learning environment combines physical device limits with an 11-variable observation vector containing storage levels, time indicators, re-newable availability, community demand, electricity prices, and outage status. 

• The controller is tested on an Australian 1,000-household case that ad-vances the authors’ earlier planning and real-time optimization studies toward policy-based operation of a PV–wind–battery–hydrogen microgrid [15, 17]. 

• Performance is examined from economic, operational, environmental, and resilience perspectives under normal conditions and three stress settings in-volving renewable variability, more frequent outages, and higher hydrogen-related costs.

2. Community Microgrid and Component Models 

The physical model follows the hourly chronology of generation, demand, storage, and grid exchange. This representation retains inter-temporal stor-age continuity and accounts for conversion efficiency and equipment con-straints at every dispatch step. 

2.1. Energy-System Configuration 

The microgrid serves an aggregated residential community using solar PV and wind generation as its principal renewable sources. A BESS provides rapid charging and discharging for short-term balancing. Longer-duration 5storage is represented by an electrolyzer, hydrogen tank, and fuel cell. Re-newable electricity remaining after the immediate load and battery-charging decision may be directed to the electrolyzer. When local electricity is scarce, hydrogen can be withdrawn from the tank and converted by the fuel cell. A bidirectional utility interface is available whenever the grid is oper-ational. Imports supply any deficit left after local generation and storage dispatch, whereas exports remove surplus electricity that cannot be con-sumed or stored within the community. The EMS therefore determines the operating pattern of the BESS, diesel generator, and hydrogen subsystem; it also respects component ratings and closes the nodal power balance through grid exchange when available.

3.6. Learning Setup 

Training comprises 300 episodes, each spanning the full 8,760-hour se-quence. Battery and hydrogen initial states are independently sampled from 20% to 80%. The discount and generalized-advantage factors are 0.995 and 0.95, respectively. Experience is gathered in horizons of 4,096 steps and pro-cessed for 10 epochs with mini-batches of 256 samples. The entropy coeffi-cient is 0.001 and the random seed is 42. Because the same annual chronology is used during learning and the normal-case assessment, that assessment is in-sample. 

4. Australian Case Study and Experimental Design 

The numerical study applies the PPO controller to the same broad plan-ning boundary and data workflow used in the preceding Australian inves-tigations, but recasts the problem as hourly policy-based dispatch. Unlike the earlier 100-household real-time test, the present model represents 1,000 homes. 

4.1. Study Location and System Scale 

The community is located in Rockhampton, Queensland, a regional Aus-tralian setting with substantial solar resource, usable wind availability, and a practical need for resilient distributed supply. Discussion 

The results confirm that PPO can produce coherent joint dispatch of the battery, hydrogen pathway, diesel generator, and grid exchange. They also show that the apparent value of each asset depends on the duration of the disturbance and on how performance is counted. Independent hourly outages were absorbed mainly by the battery and diesel unit, while fuel-cell production changed only slightly. Hydrogen may still be valuable for longer supply interruptions or extended renewable deficits, but the current Bernoulli outage process does not test that proposition. The financial result must likewise be interpreted within its stated bound-ary. The baseline A$195,690.67 is operating revenue for a system assumed to be already installed, and it is strongly influenced by the large quantity of renewable electricity exported to the grid. It is not a lifecycle profitability result because capital expenditure, component replacement, fixed operation and maintenance, detailed degradation beyond the throughput charge, and practical grid-connection limits are excluded. Training behaviour is another important qualification. Although the pol-icy eventually recovered from the collapse beginning at Episode 97, the best checkpoint remained materially better than the final episode. Future im-plementations should use validation-based checkpoint selection, smaller or better controlled updates, explicitly reported optimizer learning rates, gradi-ent clipping, and evaluation over several random seeds. Training and testing years should also be separated, and every scenario comparison should pre-serve the same initial storage states and outage sequence. Several limitations restrict broader conclusions. The high-variability se-ries is not scaled to the 1,000-household case, the random controller covers only 1,000 hours, and hydrogen price is not visible to the policy. The en-vironmental calculations use a gross-energy denominator that includes ex-ported electricity, and independent hourly outages do not reproduce a sus-tained grid failure. In addition, annual RBC, PSO, and alternative-DRL baselines have not yet been evaluated under identical conditions. Future work should address these issues, introduce realistic utility import and ex-port limits, maintain preferred storage bands, model battery and fuel-cell degradation and start-up dynamics, test multi-hour outage sequences, and link operating control with lifecycle planning [15]. 23 7. Conclusion 

A PPO-based EMS was developed for a grid-connected, hydrogen-integrated community microgrid serving 1,000 households. With the adopted operating and accounting assumptions, the baseline case produced A$195,690.67 in net annual operating revenue, a 91.2% renewable fraction, a carbon intensity of 0.085 kg CO 2/kWh, and 99.77% demand satisfac-tion. Increasing the hourly outage probability to 5% reduced revenue to A$169,892.21 and demand satisfaction to 98.79%. The controller responded primarily through the BESS and diesel generator: annual discharge and diesel output rose by 412.9% and 429.4%, whereas fuel-cell electricity increased by 7.7%. Renewable curtailment also grew from 103.76 to 578.00 MWh. Overall, the study demonstrates the promise of PPO for coordinated operation of mixed-duration storage, while establishing clear priorities for more stable training, scale-consistent stress tests, matched annual benchmarks, sustained-outage modelling, lifecycle economics, and real-time validation.
