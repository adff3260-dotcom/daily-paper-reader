Abstract 

Hydrogen-enabled community microgrids can improve renewable energy utilization and local resilience, but their operation is complicated by inter-mittent generation, uncertain residential demand, dynamic electricity prices, and the coupled dynamics of battery and hydrogen storage. This paper devel-ops a proximal policy optimization (PPO)-based energy management system (EMS) for a grid-connected community microgrid integrating photovoltaic and wind generation, battery storage, an electrolyzer, hydrogen storage, a fuel cell, and diesel backup. The EMS is formulated as a Markov decision process with an 11-dimensional state and three continuous control actions for battery, diesel, and hydrogen dispatch; grid exchange is determined from the residual power balance. The framework is evaluated using 8,760 hourly obser-vations for a 1,000-household community in Rockhampton, Australia. Under the normal operating scenario, the learned policy produced a net annual op-erating revenue of A$195,690.67, a renewable fraction of 91.2%, a carbon intensity of 0.085 kg CO 2/kWh under the adopted accounting boundary, and 99.77% load satisfaction. With the hourly grid-outage probability increased

The results demonstrate the potential of PPO for coordinated battery–hydrogen dispatch while also highlighting sensitivity to renewable-profile variability, training stability, and the choice of evaluation boundary.

1. Introduction 

1.1. Background 

Microgrids have emerged as an important architecture for decentralizing electricity supply, improving local reliability, and supporting the transition from conventional centralized grids toward low-carbon distributed energy sys-tems [1, 2, 3]. In residential and community applications, microgrids coor-dinate distributed energy resources (DERs), energy storage systems (ESSs), controllable loads, and grid exchange so that local renewable generation can be used more effectively while maintaining secure supply [4, 5]. Recent sec-toral reviews indicate that residential microgrids are no longer limited to small PV–battery installations; instead, they are evolving into multi-energy platforms that integrate solar PV, wind generation, battery energy storage systems (BESSs), electric vehicles, demand response, and hydrogen-based storage pathways [4, 6, 7]. Battery storage is widely used to smooth short-term mismatches between renewable generation and demand, support peak shaving, and improve self-consumption. However, battery-only solutions may be insufficient for pro-longed renewable shortages or seasonal energy shifting. Hydrogen technolo-gies therefore provide a complementary long-duration storage pathway, where surplus renewable electricity can be converted through electrolyzers, stored as hydrogen, and later reconverted to electricity through fuel cells [8, 9]. Recent studies further show that hydrogen-based storage can support sus-tainable and techno-economic operation of renewable-rich systems when its conversion losses and storage constraints are properly managed [10, 11]. This hybrid battery–hydrogen structure is particularly relevant for community mi-2crogrids because batteries can handle fast operational balancing, while hy-drogen storage can improve autonomy and resilience over longer time scales. The need for such coordinated storage is reinforced by the uncertainty inherent in renewable-rich community systems. Solar irradiance and wind speed fluctuate continuously, household demand varies with occupant be-havior, and electricity tariffs or grid conditions may change across operating intervals. These factors complicate real-time supply–demand balancing and make fixed dispatch rules less effective under dynamic conditions [12, 13, 14]. The related Australian residential studies further show that hybrid config-urations combining PV, wind, BESS, grid support, backup generation, and hydrogen technologies can reduce cost and emissions, but their benefits de-pend strongly on effective EMS coordination under time-varying operating conditions [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, hydrogen-enabled community microgrids require adaptive EMS frameworks capable of jointly managing renewable in-termittency, uncertain demand, short-duration battery operation, and long-duration hydrogen storage. 

1.2. Motivation 

Conventional EMS strategies are commonly based on rule-based control, deterministic scheduling, or offline optimization. Rule-based control is at-tractive because it is transparent and computationally simple, but fixed dis-patch priorities cannot fully respond to rapid changes in renewable output, load demand, electricity prices, or storage availability [18, 19, 20]. Determin-istic optimization and metaheuristic methods can improve dispatch quality by solving multi-objective cost-emission or cost-reliability problems, yet their performance depends heavily on forecast accuracy, scenario assumptions, and repeated computation whenever operating conditions change [21, 22, 23]. In renewable-rich community microgrids, these limitations become more pro-nounced because the EMS must simultaneously coordinate PV, wind, bat-teries, electrolyzers, hydrogen storage, fuel cells, grid exchange, and uncertain residential demand in near real time. Recent reviews emphasize that uncertainty-aware EMS methods, fore-casting, computational intelligence, and advanced scheduling controllers are becoming essential for microgrids with high renewable penetration and mul-tiple storage technologies [24, 25]. Broader reviews of computational intelli-gence and sustainable scheduling controllers also indicate that EMS research is moving toward adaptive controllers that can respond to renewable intermit-tency, storage dynamics, and demand variability [26, 27, 28]. The uploaded 3Australian real-time EMS study shows that PSO can outperform fixed RBC under time-varying operating conditions, but it remains an optimization pro-cedure that must be solved repeatedly and does not learn a reusable control policy from accumulated operational experience [17]. This motivates the use of deep reinforcement learning (DRL), where the EMS is formulated as an agent that interacts with the microgrid environment, observes system states, takes dispatch actions, and improves its policy through reward feedback. Recent DRL-based energy management studies demonstrate why learning-based controllers are suitable for complex sequential decisions. Expert-guided reinforcement learning has been used to improve data effi-ciency and reliability for electrified-vehicle EMSs, while MPC-assisted DRL has been proposed for fuel-cell/battery systems to improve adaptability, degradation-aware operation, and long-term decision quality [29, 30]. In demand-side and building applications, multi-agent DRL and safe DRL frameworks show how learning controllers can manage user comfort, demand response, safety, and energy efficiency under dynamic conditions [31, 32]. In port microgrids, two-layer DRL has been used to coordinate logistics–energy coupling and battery dispatch under uncertain ship arrivals [33]. In railway and urban rail applications, reward-based and PV-aware DRL methods have addressed regenerative braking, PV fluctuation, and train-load uncertainty [34, 35]. DRL has also been applied to MEC systems with distributed power sources, where task offloading and energy allocation must be coordinated with grid trading [36]. For hydrogen-enabled community microgrids, DRL is attractive because it can learn adaptive coordination among short-duration battery storage, long-duration hydrogen storage, renewable generation, and grid transactions while accounting for nonlinear device behavior and uncertain operating conditions [37, 6]. 

1.3. Research Gap 

Recent microgrid EMS reviews show that the field is moving from con-ventional rule-based and deterministic optimization methods toward data-driven, resilient, and uncertainty-aware control, but they also identify per-sistent gaps in scalability, uncertainty handling, sector-specific deployment, and integrated storage coordination [6, 4, 38]. Recent DRL applications further confirm the growing relevance of learning-based EMSs. However, expert-guided vehicle EMS and MPC–DRL fuel-cell/battery EMS studies mainly address mobility or standalone hybrid powertrain operation rather than residential community microgrids [29, 30]. Smart-home and building 4DRL studies address demand response, comfort, safety, and energy-efficiency objectives, but they do not model the coupled hydrogen-storage pathway considered here [31, 32]. Port and railway DRL studies address logistics cou-pling and regenerative braking energy storage, but they are not designed for residential hydrogen microgrids [33, 34]. MEC and urban rail DRL studies co-ordinate task offloading, distributed power, PV variability, and traction-load uncertainty; however, their constraints also differ from community micro-grids with battery, electrolyzer, hydrogen tank, fuel-cell, and grid-exchange decisions [36, 35]. The sectoral review further indicates that commercial, industrial, and residential microgrids have different operating priorities, yet many EMS studies still treat storage and demand flexibility through gener-alized formulations rather than community-scale residential operating condi-tions [4]. The related Australian residential planning study demonstrates the techno-economic and environmental value of PV, wind, battery, grid, diesel, and hydrogen configurations, but its focus is long-term sizing and scenario assessment rather than adaptive real-time control under uncertainty [15, 16]. The subsequent Australian real-time EMS study compares RBC and PSO dispatch for a hybrid residential microgrid with battery and hydrogen as-sets, showing improved cost-emission performance for PSO, while still rely-ing on predefined/metaheuristic optimization rather than a learning-based policy that can continuously adapt from interaction with uncertain operat-ing conditions [17]. Therefore, a clear research gap remains for a DRL-based EMS that jointly coordinates battery storage, electrolyzer operation, hydro-gen storage, fuel-cell dispatch, and grid exchange in a community microgrid while explicitly learning robust decisions under renewable, load, and price uncertainty. 

1.4. Contributions 

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: 

• A PPO-based EMS is developed for a hydrogen-enabled community mi-crogrid, enabling adaptive sequential decision-making under time-varying renewable generation, residential demand, electricity prices, and storage conditions. 

• A coordinated battery–hydrogen framework is formulated in which the policy controls battery, diesel, electrolyzer, and fuel-cell dispatch, while grid exchange is determined from the residual power balance. 5• The operational environment is modeled using renewable generation, com-munity load, electricity tariff, battery state of charge, hydrogen storage level, and time-related information, allowing the DRL agent to learn dis-patch policies that account for uncertainty and device constraints. 

• The proposed framework is evaluated using an Australian residential com-munity microgrid case study that extends the previous integrated techno-economic planning framework and real-time EMS studies toward learning-based operation of hybrid PV–wind–battery–hydrogen systems [15, 17]. 

• Economic, operational, environmental, and resilience behavior is quantified under normal operation, renewable-variability, grid-outage, and hydrogen-cost stress scenarios.

2. System Description 

This section defines the hydrogen-enabled community microgrid consid-ered in this study and formulates the component models required by the proposed energy management system. The system is represented through chronological power-flow relations so that renewable variability, storage dy-namics, conversion losses, and grid interactions can be captured at each op-erating interval. 

2.1. Microgrid Architecture 

The studied microgrid supplies an aggregated residential community and consists of renewable generation, short-term electrical storage, a hydrogen-based long-duration storage pathway, power-conditioning equipment, and an external grid connection. Solar photovoltaic (PV) arrays and wind turbines form the main renewable generation sources. A battery energy storage sys-tem (BESS) absorbs short-term renewable surplus and supports fast balanc-ing during load-generation mismatch. The hydrogen subsystem includes an 6electrolyzer, a hydrogen storage tank, and a fuel cell. When renewable gen-eration exceeds the immediate demand and battery-charging requirements, the electrolyzer can convert part of the surplus electrical energy into hy-drogen. During prolonged renewable shortages, the stored hydrogen can be reconverted into electricity through the fuel cell. The microgrid may also exchange power with the utility grid when the interconnection is available. Grid imports are used to cover residual deficits after local generation and storage dispatch, while grid exports allow unused renewable energy to be sold or transferred outside the community bound-ary. In this structure, the energy management system coordinates PV and wind generation, battery charging/discharging, electrolyzer operation, fuel-cell dispatch, and grid import/export decisions while respecting device limits and maintaining the power balance.

4. Case Study and Simulation Setup 

This section describes the Australian residential community microgrid used to evaluate the proposed DRL-based EMS. The case study follows the same planning boundary and data workflow adopted in the previous Aus-tralian microgrid studies, but the operating problem is reformulated here for learning-based real-time dispatch. The model represents a 1000-household community rather than the smaller 100-household model used in earlier real-time dispatch testing. 11 4.1. Australian Community Case Study 

The case study considers a grid-connected residential community micro-grid located in Rockhampton, Queensland, Australia. Rockhampton is se-lected because it represents a regional Australian community with strong solar potential, available wind resources, and practical interest in resilient distributed energy systems. The community is modeled as an aggregated residential load supplied by local renewable generation, short-duration bat-tery storage, long-duration hydrogen storage, dispatchable backup capabil-ity, and grid exchange. This boundary is consistent with the previous in-tegrated techno-economic planning framework, which evaluated hybrid PV– wind–battery–diesel–grid–hydrogen configurations for an Australian 1000-household residential community [15, 16]. Discussion 

The expanded results show that PPO coordinates battery, hydrogen, diesel, and grid exchange coherently, but the value of each resource depends on event duration and the accounting boundary. The battery and diesel generator dominate the response to independent hourly outages, whereas hydrogen output changes only modestly. Hydrogen may still support longer-duration balancing, but that proposition is not demonstrated by the present outage process. Similarly, the normal-case A$195,690.67 represents operat-ing revenue for an already installed system and is driven by large renewable exports; it is not a lifecycle profitability result because capital, replacement, fixed O&M, degradation beyond the simplified throughput charge, and grid-connection limits are excluded. The training record is important for interpreting performance. Although the policy recovers after the Episode 97 collapse, the best checkpoint remains substantially better than the final episode. More conservative clipping, ex-plicit optimizer learning rates, gradient clipping, checkpoint validation, and multiple random seeds should therefore be used in the next model revision. Evaluation should also separate training and testing years and preserve iden-tical initial states and outage traces across scenario comparisons. Several limitations constrain cross-scenario conclusions. The synthetic high-variability profile is not scaled to the 1,000-household case; the random policy spans only 1,000 hours; hydrogen cost is not observable by the policy; and environmental intensity and avoided-emissions calculations use a gross-energy denominator that includes exported energy. In addition, independent Bernoulli outages do not reproduce sustained grid failures, and no annual RBC, PSO, or alternative-DRL benchmark is currently available. Future work should address these limitations, impose realistic import/export limits, enforce preferred storage bands, include battery and fuel-cell degradation and startup dynamics, and integrate operational control with lifecycle planning [15]. 

7. Conclusion 

This paper developed a PPO-based EMS for a hydrogen-enabled micro-grid serving 1,000 households. Normal operation achieved A$195,690.67 in net operating revenue, a 91.2% renewable fraction, 0.085 kg CO 2/kWh carbon intensity under the adopted gross-energy boundary, and 99.77% demand sat-isfaction. At 5% hourly outage probability, revenue remained A$169,892.21 24 and demand satisfaction was 98.79%; battery discharge and diesel genera-tion increased by 412.9% and 429.4%, respectively, while hydrogen output increased by 7.7%. Outages also increased renewable curtailment from 103.76 to 578.00 MWh. The results demonstrate PPO’s potential for coordinated operation while motivating stabilized training, scale-matched uncertainty tests, annual controller benchmarks, multi-hour outage modeling, lifecycle economics, and real-time validation.
